Breast Screening Follow-up Agent

The problem

Women with an abnormal mammogram sometimes get lost before follow-up, especially older women. Sometimes the finding is in both breasts and she only hears about one side. Sometimes the message gets lost between the radiologist, her doctor and her.

This agent follows every finding, on each side, until it's resolved. Rules read the reports, set the deadlines and decide every follow-up. Claude helps where judgment is needed: it reads her text replies, and it checks each biopsy result against the imaging. People make the clinical calls.

What is a rule, and what needs judgment

The product manager's job here is to ask radiologists and surgeons what has gone wrong before, and build for that. Then decide which parts should be rules and which parts need judgment.

The BI-RADS category and the deadlines are rules. A report states its category in a fixed place, so code reads it the same way every time, and a report that doesn't fit goes to a radiologist instead of a guess. Comparing the radiology and pathology reports needs judgment. A benign biopsy can still be wrong when it doesn't explain what the imaging showed, like a spiculated mass. That's where Claude helps. It puts the two reports side by side and flags what may not match, and the radiologist decides.

How it works

Rules read and decide. Claude helps with judgment. People make the clinical calls.

  1. A new report arrives. This starts the agent.
  2. Rules read the report. They pull out the BI-RADS category, the exam date, which breast and the recommendation, each with the exact words it came from. When the report gives each breast its own category, they read each one.
  3. A report that doesn't fit the rules goes to a radiologist. No category, two categories for one breast, or a negative impression over findings that describe a mass: the case stops there until a radiologist looks at it.
  4. Rules pick the pathway and set a due date for each breast. See the table below. When each breast has its own assessment, each breast gets its own case, so a finding on one side can't hide the other.
  5. The agent waits and watches. Each day it checks whether the next step was booked or done.
  6. If the next step isn't booked, the agent reaches out. It texts a booking link and checks the schedule. Claude reads her replies. No ride means a free ride option, and another time means new slots. No answer means a portal message, then an automated call.
  7. A person steps in only for exceptions. She says she is scared or asks a medical question, turns down care, raises a barrier the agent can't solve, or doesn't answer after three tries.
  8. A new result comes in and the loop starts again. A diagnostic mammogram goes back through steps 2 to 4.
  9. When a biopsy result comes back, Claude compares it with the imaging. It writes a short side-by-side summary and flags anything that may not match, with the exact words from both reports. A radiologist decides. If it doesn't match, the agent follows her to a repeat biopsy.
  10. The case closes when the result is resolved. Back to routine screening, on a six-month track, or handed to breast surgery after a cancer diagnosis.

The outer limit is 60 days from the abnormal result to resolution, the window a national quality measure uses. The deadlines above are set so a case never gets near it. For BI-RADS 3 there is time, so unanswered reminders keep going until one month after the due date.

A person decides any report the rules can't read cleanly, the wording of each message type (approved once before launch), whether a biopsy result matches the imaging, and any case at the navigator stage. The agent never changes a BI-RADS category, orders a test or tells a patient a result.

The rules that read each report
  • Category: the BI-RADS category in the impression. One per report, or one per breast when each impression line names its breast. An overall assessment can't be less urgent than either breast.
  • Exam date: the DATE line. An addendum counts from the exam it corrects.
  • Breast: from the EXAM line, or from the recommendation when the exam line doesn't say.
  • Next step: the recommendation, for each breast when it names them.
  • Doesn't add up: a BI-RADS 1 report whose findings describe a mass, asymmetry, calcifications or distortion that isn't ruled out.
  • Pathology: cancer, atypia, benign or nondiagnostic, from the DIAGNOSIS line, skipping what it rules out (like "negative for malignancy"). BI-RADS 5 imaging with a benign result is always flagged.
  • Anything else that doesn't fit goes to a radiologist, with the reason.
The prompt Claude gets to compare a biopsy result with the imaging 
You are helping a breast radiologist with radiology-pathology correlation. You get two reports about the same patient: the breast imaging report that led to a biopsy, and the pathology report from that biopsy.

Write a short chart summary that puts the two side by side, and point out anything that may not match. You do not decide. The radiologist decides whether the result matches the imaging. You never write to the patient.

What to return:
- imaging: the biopsied finding as the imaging report describes it. Its breast ("left" or "right"), its size, its margins or shape, and its BI-RADS category. Copy the words for size, margins and BI-RADS into "quotes" exactly as they appear in the imaging report. Use null for anything the report doesn't say.
- pathology: the diagnosis in plain words, and "quote", the diagnosis copied exactly from the pathology report.
- match: "concordant" when the diagnosis is a reasonable explanation for the imaging finding. "possible_mismatch" when it may not be. "unclear" when the reports don't give enough to compare.
- flags: one entry for each concern. Each has "concern", one plain sentence, plus "imaging_quote" and "pathology_quote", the exact words from each report that the concern rests on. Leave the list empty when nothing looks off.
- summary: two or three plain sentences a radiologist can read in ten seconds.

How to judge:
1. A benign result that doesn't specifically explain a suspicious finding is a possible mismatch. Suspicious means things like an irregular shape, spiculated, angular or indistinct margins, architectural distortion, or BI-RADS 4C or 5. A specific benign diagnosis that fits the finding, like a fibroadenoma in an oval circumscribed mass, usually matches.
2. Flag it if the reports describe a different breast or a different location.
3. A cancer diagnosis in a suspicious finding matches. Say so plainly.

Rules:
1. Copy every quote exactly, character for character. Code checks each quote against the report it came from, and a quote that doesn't match is thrown out.
2. Keep dates out of the quotes and the summary.
3. Don't recommend treatment and don't tell anyone a result.

Reply with only a JSON object in this shape, no other text:
{"imaging": {"side": "left", "size": "...", "margins": "...", "birads": "4C", "quotes": {"size": "exact words", "margins": "exact words", "birads": "exact words"}}, "pathology": {"diagnosis": "...", "quote": "exact words"}, "match": "concordant", "flags": [{"concern": "...", "imaging_quote": "exact words", "pathology_quote": "exact words"}], "summary": "..."}
The prompt Claude gets for each patient reply 
You are reading a patient's text-message reply for a breast imaging follow-up agent. The agent had texted her a link to book her next breast imaging appointment or biopsy.

Say what she is telling us. You do not reply to her and you do not decide what happens next. Code does that after you.

Pick every label that applies:
- booked: she says she booked or scheduled
- wants_new_time: she asks for a different day or time
- transport_barrier: she has no way to get to the appointment
- other_barrier: another practical problem, such as cost, childcare or work
- scared_or_worried: she says she is afraid, anxious or worried
- medical_question: she asks a medical question
- declines_care: she says she does not want the test or procedure
- wrong_number: the message reached the wrong person
- other: none of the above

Copy "quote" word for word from her message, the shortest span that supports the labels.

Reply with only a JSON object in this shape, no other text:
{"labels": ["..."], "quote": "exact words", "summary": "one short plain-language sentence"}

What a real deployment would add

  • Real connections: the scheduling system, two-way texting, and the EHR inbox in place of simulated events.
  • Running the report rules on a few hundred real reports from each site, and adding a rule wherever a site's format doesn't fit.
  • Checking Claude's mismatch flags against radiologists' calls, to see how often a flag is right and whether any mismatch slips through.
  • No-shows and rebooking, plus a language preference for every message.
  • The 60-day rate and days to diagnosis reported per site every week.
